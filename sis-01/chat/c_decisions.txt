(1) Empty reserved rooms: accepted. The draft must address them, but a no-show rule (for example releasing a room nobody checked into) is the administrator's policy decision, not ours; version 1 only has to make no-shows visible. (2) AI per principle: accepted. Say for each of the four principles what an AI can support and what people must decide. (3) Who owns the rules: accepted. Booking rules are faculty policy owned by the facilities administrator; the developers elicit, record, implement and test them and decide technical risk. "Signing off" means the administrator accepts the release. (4) Course wording of the principles: accepted after checking Sommerville (evidence 1). Process means a planned, managed process, not only a model choice; dependability includes availability and performance. (5) Waterfall as a strawman: qualified. Keep incremental, but add a paper prototype shown to the administrator in weeks 1–2 to settle most rules before building; only rules that need real use stay open (evidence 3). (6) Deferring blocking and the paper sheets: accepted. Blocking moves into the first increment because exam rooms are blocked in exam week, and the sheets are removed on launch day so there is one source of truth. (7) Timing and usage reports: qualified. Version 1 must be live well before exam week (fictional plan: week 8 of 12). Usage reports are not invented: usage review is an administrator story in our Week 03 requirements and it is how no-shows become visible, so they stay in a later increment. (8) "Errors sit at the boundaries" overclaims: accepted. In our Week 05 lab six faulty versions survived the six table tests, and the two that survived longest were not boundary bugs: they sorted or appended to the list of existing bookings. Containment must be tested too. The 120-minute limit is a duration rule from the Week 05 contract, not part of the overlap check. (9) Fail-closed and availability: qualified. Drop the sentence about rejecting a booking when the check cannot complete; it adds nothing. Accept that availability is part of dependability (evidence 1). Reject "accept and flag the conflict": a double booking in exam week is exactly the risk we named, and a database constraint rejects the second booking cheaply. (10) Detection and recovery: qualified. With a database constraint, overlapping rows cannot be stored, so a nightly check is not needed for new data; detection matters once, when the bookings from the paper sheets are entered at launch, because the constraint cannot be created over overlapping rows. (11) Simultaneous requests: accepted. They are normal in exam week, a unique constraint on start time is not enough, and the database rule must be named (evidence 2). (12) Maintainability and privacy: accepted, briefly. The two developers will graduate, so the rules table and tests are the handover; other students see "booked", not who booked. (13) Backward traceability: accepted. Each rule gets a source column (who asked for it); a rule with no source is an invented rule. This is our defence against invented rules. (14) R3 one-sided, change control: qualified. Drop the R3 example rather than expand it; change control is covered by decision 3 (the administrator approves rule changes). (15) SSO is a requirement, not a reuse choice: accepted. The money claim is wrong: writing our own login would cost time, not budget. The bigger reuse question is whether an existing open-source booking system fits; I have not verified any specific product, so it stays an open check, not a recommendation. (16) "AI-generated code is reuse" challenged: rejected. Our course explicitly calls using a code-generating model a form of reuse (evidence 3). Accepted only the point that once merged, the maintainer is us. (17) "Rules nobody asked for": accepted. The scenario does say the administrator blocks rooms; the AI added "only" (access control resting on authentication, which was out of scope), and it invented the cancellation rule outright. Frame it as the AI filling gaps in an underspecified prompt, which is evidence for requirements management. (18) Correlated errors: accepted. If the same AI writes the check and its tests, the tests can share its misunderstanding; independence comes from tests derived from the acceptance criteria, as in our Week 05 lab where expected values came from the criteria, never from running the code.
